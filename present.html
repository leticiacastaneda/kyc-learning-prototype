These causes are my reading of the symptoms in the brief. They are hypotheses we confirm with the client before acting.

The question behind each symptom

If people had to do it right, could they? If yes, the problem isn't skill: it's motivation, tools or leadership. That logic follows performance-analysis models such as Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model and Mager & Pipe.

How we confirm it in weeks 1–3

  • Anonymous survey on tracked reversions
  • Separate interviews with experts, new analysts and managers, never mixed
  • Manager calibration on the same files, to measure inconsistency
  • Case-based assessment for every analyst, with a confidence rating

Why experts revert: four possible causes

Fluency (working hypothesis), a real flaw in the process, loss of identity or autonomy, or no reinforcement. The decision gate in week 3 decides which one (slide 5).

Act 2 · The solution

I tested the diagnosis against what worked elsewhere

Different industries, same pattern: a correct process that people didn't follow. The evidence confirmed the design; it didn't replace it.

1
Fix the system before the course

Harvard research: people trained and sent back to an unchanged system don't apply what they learned.

In the planManagers and guides change before training starts
2
Learn from the teams that already succeed

U.S. veterans' hospitals let frontline staff design the practices: ICU infections fell 62%.

In the planExpert referents build the practice cases
3
Leaders decide whether a change sticks

16 hospitals learned one new surgery: by the 40th case, 143 vs 305 minutes. How leaders framed it explained the gap.

In the planManagers calibrated first, then a weekly review
  • Fix the system before the course. Beer, HBS Working Knowledge / HBR.
  • Put the help where the work happens. A one-page surgical checklist in 8 hospitals across 8 countries cut major complications from 11% to 7%. Haynes et al., NEJM 2009. In the plan: one-page step guides inside the workflow.
  • Learn from the teams that already succeed. 62% fewer ICU infections, 45% elsewhere. Jain et al., NEJM 2011.
  • Treat resistance as information. The loudest critics often see real flaws in the plan. Ford & Ford, HBR 2009. In the plan: no-blame friction channel; experts join the diagnosis.
  • Leaders decide whether a change sticks. Edmondson, Bohmer & Pisano, ASQ 2001; Pisano et al., Management Science 2001.

Diagnosis first, from the brief and my own change work. Then I checked each hypothesis against original studies. Every figure traces to the primary source; one secondary-source figure was corrected.

  • Kirkpatrick: learning, behavior and results (levels 2–4). Reaction surveys are not the evidence.
  • Prosci adoption factors: speed, utilization and proficiency.
  • ADKAR assessment: where each group is stuck (slide 10).
  • Certainty-based marking: confidence ratings catch confident mistakes, the riskiest ones in compliance.
  • Success Case Method (Brinkerhoff): interviews with the best and worst teams explain the numbers.

Why these three rules

  • Adherence alone can be gamed: a fast, “compliant” file can still be wrong.
  • Penalties while learning push errors underground.
  • Targets set without the client aren't owned by the client.

Why this objective

It attacks the one cause training can solve alone (“not sure I apply it correctly”). In the scenario, a mismatched file comes back from Quality: rework, delay and compliance risk. I chose it over an approve / request / escalate objective because it is clearer to demonstrate and closer to the errors that send files back. The decision is still practiced in stage 4.

Facts

  • Who: analysts whose assessment showed gaps in this step
  • Format: self-paced, desktop or mobile, real-looking sample files
  • Reviewed by: process owner, Compliance, two expert referents
  • Size: 16 screens across five stages

Assessment

Instant feedback in practice. In the check, learners rate their certainty. Results by check (mastered / correct but unsure / keep practicing / priority), never one score, never used in performance reviews.

Accessibility · WCAG 2.2 AA

Keyboard and list alternative to every click-on-image task, alt text, captions, no color-only cues, no time limits, 200% zoom, mobile.

Privacy

Learner sees detail; manager sees required workshops; client sees totals by step and team.

My habit: ask of every claim whether it is a fact, an inference or a decision we made.

Where AI went wrong in this project

  • Follows instructions literally: “up to 5 slides” became five dense slides. Habit: review as the end user would.
  • Presents inferences as facts: design rationales and a claim about the sponsor appeared undiscussed. Habit: fact, inference or decision?
  • Leans on secondary or outdated sources: a wrong case figure, an outdated palette. Habit: require the original source.
  • Volume before clarity: complete but text-heavy drafts. Habit: objective critique, then one idea per section.

Rules I'd agree with a regulated client

  • No real client data in AI tools
  • Experts and Compliance validate everything
  • Every claim is traceable
  • Check accessibility and bias
  • Be transparent about AI use

Tool: Claude (Anthropic), as research, critique and drafting partner, with a running decision log.
